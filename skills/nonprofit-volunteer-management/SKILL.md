# Nonprofit Volunteer Management

## Boundaries

Covers unpaid service volunteers: event, program, pro bono skilled, and episodic/micro-volunteers,
including volunteer program value reporting.

- Governing board member recruitment and onboarding: `nonprofit-board-recruitment`.
- Paid staff hiring, compensation, or workplace culture: `nonprofit-hr`, `nonprofit-staff-retention`.
- Congregational lay leadership and ministry volunteers: `nonprofit-faith-volunteer-lay-leadership`.
- Construction and home-repair build-site volunteers: `nonprofit-housing-construction-volunteers`.
- Peer-to-peer campaigns where "volunteers" are really fundraisers:
  `nonprofit-peer-to-peer-fundraising`.
- Insurance and liability review of screening gaps: `nonprofit-risk-management`.

## Core Framework: The Volunteer Life Cycle

Treat every engagement as a pipeline with six stages — mirroring donor moves management but for time instead of money. Deliverables and failure modes cluster by stage:

1. **Plan** — Define the need before recruiting. For every open role, produce a **volunteer position description** (template below). Rule of thumb: if you can't write a position description, you aren't ready to recruit — this is the #1 failure mode (recruiting generically, then improvising roles on arrival, which drives early attrition).
2. **Recruit** — Match the channel to the role. High-skill/pro bono roles (board-adjacent, finance, marketing) come from LinkedIn, VolunteerMatch, Catchafire, corporate skills-based volunteering programs, and personal asks. High-volume/low-skill/episodic roles (event day, sorting, mailing) come from corporate group volunteer days, university service programs, Idealist, JustServe, faith communities, and word-of-mouth from current volunteers (the single highest-converting channel — always ask current volunteers to refer one person). Track a recruitment funnel with explicit conversion rates: inquiries → applications → interviews → placements → active after 90 days. A funnel with high inquiries but low 90-day retention signals an onboarding problem, not a recruitment problem — diagnose the right stage before changing tactics.
3. **Screen** — Match screening intensity to risk, not to organizational habit. Use a tiered model: Tier 1 (low-risk, one-time/event, supervised) = application only; Tier 2 (recurring, unsupervised contact with clients) = application + interview + 2 references; Tier 3 (unsupervised contact with minors, elderly, or vulnerable adults, or financial access) = application + interview + references + criminal background check (and in the US, a check against the sex offender registry) + fingerprinting where state law or funder/insurer requires it (confirm current state requirements and insurer terms before finalizing the policy). Document the screening tier decision for every role in a policy, not case-by-case — inconsistent screening is a top liability and insurance-audit finding.
4. **Onboard** — Run orientation (mission, programs, policies, safety/emergency procedures, chain of command) separately from role-specific training (how to do the specific task). Give every volunteer a written handbook or one-pager covering: code of conduct, confidentiality expectations, attendance/no-show policy, dress code/ID badge, incident reporting process, and who to call with questions. Assign a first-shift buddy or mentor — first-shift experience is the single biggest predictor of a second shift.
5. **Support & Schedule** — Choose a scheduling mechanism sized to volume: simple sign-up sheets or a shared calendar for small programs; a dedicated volunteer management system (e.g., Golden, VolunteerHub, Better Impact, Galaxy Digital, Civic Champs, or a CRM module; verify current vendor offerings, ownership, and pricing before recommending one) once you exceed roughly 50–75 active volunteers or need automated reminders, hour tracking, and reporting. Whatever the tool, build in automated shift reminders (24–48 hours ahead) and a simple no-show/cancellation protocol — most no-shows are preventable with a reminder, not a motivation problem.
6. **Recognize & Retain** — Recognition must be layered, not a once-a-year banquet: (a) immediate/in-the-moment thanks after each shift, (b) milestone recognition tied to service hours or tenure (e.g., 25/100/500-hour pins or certificates), (c) an annual recognition event, and (d) meaningful-role recognition — publicly connecting a volunteer's specific contribution to a specific outcome, not generic gratitude. Track retention as a rate (active volunteers at month 12 ÷ volunteers onboarded 12 months ago) and diagnose drop-off points (post-first-shift, post-90-days, post-one-year) separately, because each has a different fix (first-shift = onboarding/buddy system; 90-day = role fit/supervisor relationship; one-year = burnout/recognition/growth opportunity).

## Procedure

1. Inventory current roles; write or refresh a position description for each.
2. Set a screening tier per role and put it in a written policy so intake staff apply it
   consistently.
3. Build the application form: contact info, availability, skills/interests, emergency contact, and
   consent for the screening tier of the roles applied for.
4. Onboarding sequence: pre-arrival welcome email → orientation → role-specific training → first
   shift with a buddy → 2-week check-in call.
5. Pick a scheduling mechanism sized to volume (Stage 5) and set up automated reminders.
6. Track three numbers monthly — new applicants, active volunteers, volunteers lost (with exit
   reason) — and calculate 90-day and 12-month retention.
7. Put the layered recognition dates (Stage 6) on the annual calendar now.
8. For attrition: pull funnel numbers, find the stage where the drop concentrates, and apply that
   stage's fix rather than a blanket "do more recognition."
9. To report program value, multiply volunteer hours by the current Independent Sector
   value-of-volunteer-time rate for your state/year, and pair it with 2–3 outcome stories and a
   capacity argument ("volunteers add the equivalent of X FTEs the budget doesn't cover") — dollar
   value alone invites a "just replace them with money" misread.

## Position Description Template

```
Volunteer Position Description — [Role title]
Purpose / impact: [how this role advances the mission, one or two sentences]
Duties: [3-6 specific tasks]
Time commitment: [hours/week], [shift length], [duration or end date]
Location / remote: [ ]
Skills required: [ ]        Training provided: [ ]
Screening tier: [1 / 2 / 3] — [what that requires, per screening policy]
Supervisor: [name, title, contact]
What you receive: [training, references, service-hour certification, other]
Contact to apply: [ ]
```

## Verify Before Acting

Before publishing a screening policy or role description:

- [ ] Every role is assigned a screening tier in the written policy, not case-by-case.
- [ ] Every role with unsupervised access to minors, elderly, or vulnerable adults, or financial
      access, is Tier 3.
- [ ] Background-check, registry, and fingerprinting steps match current state law and the
      insurer's or funder's requirements.
- [ ] Each position description names a supervisor and a time commitment.
- [ ] Any value-of-volunteer-time figure uses the current published rate for the right state and year.

## For advisors

- Start with a program audit (position descriptions, screening policy, retention rate, funnel
  numbers); building the tracking is often the first deliverable. Frame maturity as ad hoc →
  structured → strategic and recommend the smallest change set that moves one stage.
- Flag screening gaps (e.g., unsupervised access to minors with no background check) immediately as
  a risk-management issue and involve `nonprofit-risk-management`; don't wait for the engagement to
  finish.
- When retention is the complaint, insist on funnel data first; prescribing a recognition event when
  the real leak is a broken first-shift experience is a common consulting failure.

## Common Failure Modes

- Recruiting before defining the role, leading to volunteer disillusionment and early drop-off.
- One-size-fits-all screening (either over-screening low-risk event volunteers, which suppresses recruitment, or under-screening high-risk roles, which creates real liability).
- No systematic reminder or scheduling process, causing no-shows that get misdiagnosed as a motivation/commitment problem.
- Recognition treated as a single annual event rather than a layered, ongoing practice.
- Measuring only headcount recruited, never retention rate or funnel conversion, so the same recruitment gap gets "solved" every year without fixing the leak.
