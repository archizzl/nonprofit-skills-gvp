# Corporate Sponsorships & Cause Marketing

## Boundaries

- Corporate *foundation* or CSR *grant* funding (no marketing exchange, application-based):
  `nonprofit-grant-research`.
- The underlying event's budget and logistics: `nonprofit-fundraising-events`.
- Performing-arts season and production sponsorship: `nonprofit-arts-season-sponsorship`.

## Sponsorship vs. Grant vs. Donation

Frame this distinction explicitly with clients/staff: a sponsorship is a **transaction** where the
business receives marketing value (logo placement, attendee access, hospitality, co-branded
content) roughly proportionate to their payment — this has tax and accounting implications
(sponsorship payments with substantial return benefit may not be fully deductible as a charitable
contribution to the sponsor, and may create unrelated business income tax (UBIT) exposure for the
nonprofit if benefits exceed IRS qualified sponsorship payment thresholds — flag this for finance
review, especially for high-value in-kind benefit packages; verify the current qualified
sponsorship rules and thresholds in IRS guidance; do not give definitive tax advice). Conflating
sponsorships and grants in the org's own tracking and gift-acceptance policy causes downstream tax
and donor-recognition confusion.

## Sponsorship Tier Design

1. Define tiers (commonly 3-5: e.g., Presenting/Title, Gold, Silver, Bronze, In-Kind) each with a
   price point and a specific benefits list.
2. Price the top tier first, anchored to the value of what only that sponsor gets (naming rights,
   stage time, top logo placement) and to comparable market rates for similar-sized organizations
   — then price down, ensuring each lower tier is a clear, visibly smaller bundle (not just a
   smaller logo) to make upgrading obviously attractive.
3. Allocate benefit levers across tiers: logo placement, verbal/stage recognition, tickets/tables,
   VIP hospitality, speaking or branded materials, social mentions/co-branded content, category
   exclusivity (e.g., "presenting sponsor in the beverage category" excludes competitors), and a
   post-event impact report.
4. Reserve category exclusivity and naming rights only for the top 1-2 tiers — over-granting
   exclusivity at lower price points erodes what top sponsors are paying for.
5. Include an in-kind/gift-in-kind tier for goods/services donations (auction items, printing,
   catering) valued at a benefits-equivalent level, distinct from cash tiers.

## Pitch Deck / Proposal Structure

Lead with audience fit, not org history:

1. Brief credibility and mission snapshot.
2. Audience/reach data (attendee count and demographics, email and social reach, media history) —
   sponsors are buying access to this audience.
3. Tier grid for side-by-side comparison.
4. A specific, named ask tied to the prospect's own business (e.g., "as a fitting Presenting
   Sponsor for our 5K given your retail presence in this community") rather than a generic deck.
5. Past sponsor logos/testimonials, if available, and one clear next step and point of contact.

## Standard Deliverables

- **Sponsorship prospectus** — the full tier grid and benefits document used across all pitches.
- **Custom pitch deck/one-pager** per major prospect, tailored to their brand/category fit.
- **Sponsorship agreement/letter of agreement** — specifying payment terms, deliverables owed by
  both parties (what the org will provide, by when), usage rights for the sponsor's logo/brand, and
  a cancellation/refund clause.
- **Fulfillment checklist** — internal tracker ensuring every promised benefit (logo placement,
  tickets, stage mention, social post) is actually delivered and dated; this is the most common
  point of sponsor dissatisfaction if missed.
- **Renewal packet** — prior-year fulfillment proof (photos, impressions/reach data, impact
  report) plus next year's ask, sent well before the renewal decision window.

## Concrete Steps

1. Design or refresh the tier structure and pricing before approaching any prospect.
2. Build the sponsorship prospectus and a fulfillment checklist template.
3. Prioritize prospects by category fit and existing relationship (a warm board/staff connection
   converts far better than a cold approach) — coordinate sourcing with existing donor/business
   relationships in the pipeline system if applicable.
4. Customize the pitch deck to each top-tier prospect's brand and community footprint; make a
   specific, named tier ask rather than "let us know what works for you."
5. Negotiate and document terms in a signed sponsorship agreement before the event/program begins.
6. Execute fulfillment against the checklist in real time; capture proof (photos, screenshots,
   analytics) for the renewal packet.
7. Send a post-event/program impact report to every sponsor within 2-4 weeks, quantifying their
   exposure/reach where possible.
8. Approach renewal 2-3 months ahead of the next cycle's deadline, leading with fulfillment proof
   from the prior year before making the next ask.
9. Track year-over-year sponsor retention rate as a core metric; investigate any non-renewal
   directly rather than assuming it was budget-driven.

## Verify Before Acting

Before the prospectus or any agreement goes out:

- [ ] Every benefit beyond acknowledgment (advertising language, endorsements, substantial return
  benefits such as high-value hospitality) is flagged for finance review against current IRS
  qualified sponsorship guidance.
- [ ] Each lower tier is a visibly smaller bundle than the one above it, and exclusivity or naming
  rights appear only in the top 1-2 tiers.
- [ ] New category-exclusivity grants are checked against existing sponsor agreements.
- [ ] Every benefit promised in the proposal appears in the agreement and in the fulfillment
  checklist with an owner and date.
- [ ] The agreement covers payment terms, both parties' deliverables, logo usage rights and
  cancellation/refund.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Underpricing top tiers**: setting Presenting/Title sponsorship too low relative to the
  exclusivity and visibility it grants, leaving revenue on the table.
- **Benefit creep**: granting one sponsor extra perks outside their tier informally, which
  undermines the tier structure and creates inconsistent expectations across sponsors.
- **No fulfillment tracking**: promising benefits in the proposal that never get delivered (a
  missed logo placement, an unfulfilled stage mention) — the single most common cause of
  non-renewal. When revenue is flat or sponsors aren't renewing, audit fulfillment records before
  touching the pitch deck or pricing.
- **Treating sponsorship like a grant**: sending a sponsor a generic thank-you letter instead of
  quantified marketing/reach value, missing the business case for renewal.
- **Ignoring exclusivity conflicts**: signing two competing-category sponsors without checking
  prior exclusivity commitments.
- **No tax/UBIT review**: designing a high-value benefits package without flagging potential
  deductibility or UBIT implications to finance/legal.
