# Nonprofit Succession Planning

## Boundaries

Covers ED/CEO succession (and by extension other single-point-of-failure senior roles) from the
board's seat: the emergency plan, the long-range plan, readiness, and the internal pipeline.

- Running the search — committee charter, position profile, comp benchmark, interviews, offer:
  `nonprofit-executive-search`.
- The interim or acting ED's workplan and mandate, the outgoing ED's handoff memo, and the
  incoming executive's first 90 days: `nonprofit-executive-transitions`.
- Communication and org-design execution once a transition is publicly underway:
  `nonprofit-change-management`.
- Non-executive staff morale, burnout, or turnover: `nonprofit-staff-retention`.
- Board composition gaps the transition surfaces (e.g., no HR/search expertise):
  `nonprofit-board-recruitment`.

## Core Framework: Two Plans, Not One

Nonprofit succession planning literature (see Annie E. Casey Foundation and BoardSource succession planning guidance) draws a sharp line between two distinct plans that are often conflated — conflating them is itself a common failure mode:

1. **Emergency succession plan (departure risk plan).** A short, standing document — every organization should have one regardless of the ED's current health or tenure — answering: who is the immediate acting authority if the ED cannot serve starting tomorrow (usually the deputy director, COO, or a named senior staff member, ratified in advance by board vote); what decisions that acting leader can and cannot make without board approval (e.g., signing authority thresholds, hiring/firing, contracts); who on the board is the point of contact; and where key passwords/accounts/signatory documents are stored. Review and re-ratify this annually regardless of other activity — a stale emergency plan (naming someone who has since left) is worse than none, because it creates false confidence.
2. **Strategic/long-range succession plan.** Activated 1–3 years before a known departure (planned retirement, term-limited tenure, or founder transition). This is a full project with its own timeline, not a document filed away: (a) board forms a transition/search committee distinct from the standing governance committee; (b) organizational readiness assessment — is the org financially stable enough, does it depend too heavily on the outgoing ED's personal relationships (a **founder's syndrome** risk flag: donor relationships, program credibility, or institutional memory concentrated in one person with nothing documented); (c) internal candidate development if a pipeline exists — assess readiness against a leadership competency framework, not just tenure; (d) decision on internal promotion vs. open search vs. interim-then-search; (e) search process and timeline (typically 4–9 months for an ED search; run the search itself with `nonprofit-executive-search`); (f) transition period design — outgoing ED's role during the search (advisor vs. fully stepped back), interim leadership arrangement if a gap exists, and a defined handoff period (knowledge transfer, donor/funder introductions, staff introductions) capped at a specific length so it doesn't drift into shadow leadership that undermines the incoming ED's authority. The interim's own workplan and the outgoing ED's handoff memo are `nonprofit-executive-transitions`.

Founder transitions deserve their own risk flag: when the departing ED is also the founder, board and staff loyalty, donor relationships, and organizational identity are often entangled with that individual specifically. Name this explicitly in the readiness assessment and plan extra time and structure (e.g., a founder-emeritus or advisory role with clearly bounded authority, explicit donor re-introduction plan) rather than assuming a standard transition timeline applies.

## Procedure

1. **Emergency plan first.** Confirm one exists and is current; if not, draft it now (template
   below) and get it ratified by full board vote — independent of any known departure.
2. **Transition committee.** If a departure is 1–3 years out (or contemplated), form a small
   committee (typically 3–5 board members, with senior staff input in later stages), separate from
   the standing governance committee and routine business, to keep the process focused and
   confidential where needed.
3. **Readiness assessment.** Financial position, dependency on the outgoing ED's personal
   donor/funder relationships, documentation of institutional knowledge (SOPs, funder history,
   program history), and staff leadership bench strength. Name founder's-syndrome or
   donor-concentration risk to the board directly, even when uncomfortable.
4. **Internal candidates.** Assess against a written leadership competency framework (strategic
   thinking, board relations, fundraising capacity, financial management, program oversight, people
   management) and build development plans (stretch assignments, mentoring by the outgoing ED,
   external training) well before the transition — pipeline development takes 1–2 years minimum to
   be credible.
5. **Decide the path** and document it: internal promotion, open external search, or
   interim-then-search. If interim leadership is needed, define scope, authority, and an end date
   in writing (detailed workplan via `nonprofit-executive-transitions`).
6. **Realistic timeline.** Budget 4–9 months for an ED search, longer for founder transitions;
   anxious boards routinely compress timelines, and a rushed search is a common cause of a failed
   placement within 18 months. Hand search mechanics to `nonprofit-executive-search`.
7. **Handoff boundaries.** Define what the outgoing ED will and will not do post-departure (board
   seat, advisory role, no role) and a specific donor/funder re-introduction plan so relationships
   transfer deliberately.
8. **Founder transitions.** Name the founder's-syndrome risk to the board and build in extra runway
   and structure (bounded advisory role, explicit relationship-transfer plan) rather than treating
   it as a standard ED swap.

## Emergency Succession Plan Template

```
Emergency Succession Plan — [Organization]          Ratified by board vote: [date]  Next review: [date]
1. Trigger: ED unable to serve (illness, accident, abrupt departure) for [period] or longer
2. Acting authority: [name, title]; backup: [name, title]
3. Acting leader may: [day-to-day operations; sign up to $[threshold]; ...]
   Acting leader may not without board approval: [hire/fire senior staff; contracts over $[ ]; ...]
4. Board point of contact: [board chair name, phone]; backup: [vice chair]
5. Signatory changes needed: [bank, payroll, grants portals] — owner: [treasurer]
6. Key accounts and documents: [where stored; who holds access — not the passwords themselves]
7. Who is told, in what order: [board, staff, top funders] — drafts via `nonprofit-executive-communications`
8. Path to longer-term decision: board meets within [n] days to decide interim vs. search
```

## Verify Before Acting

Before the board ratifies a succession plan:

- [ ] Every named person (acting leader, backup, board contact) is still in role and has agreed.
- [ ] Authority limits and signing thresholds are explicit and match the bylaws and financial
      controls policy.
- [ ] Account and document locations are current; no passwords are written into the plan itself.
- [ ] The board vote and date are recorded in the minutes, with an annual review date set.
- [ ] Emergency and long-range plans are separate documents.

## For advisors

- Ask first whether an emergency plan exists — often absent even in well-run organizations, and a
  fast, low-cost first deliverable.
- Naming founder's-syndrome or donor-concentration risk candidly is often the highest-value
  insight an outside advisor provides; insiders are too close to name it.
- Keep your deliverable to the succession plan and process design; coordinate the communications
  rollout with `nonprofit-change-management`.

## Common Failure Modes

- No emergency succession plan at all, discovered only after a sudden departure when it's too late to plan calmly.
- Conflating the emergency plan with the strategic plan, producing one thin document that serves neither purpose well.
- Starting strategic succession planning too late (months, not years, before a known departure), leaving no time for internal candidate development.
- Ignoring founder's-syndrome risk — assuming a founder transition can run on the same timeline and structure as a standard ED swap.
- An open-ended "interim" arrangement with no defined end date or scope, creating prolonged ambiguity.
- No structured donor/funder relationship transfer plan, causing major gift and grant relationships to lapse with the departing ED.
- Letting the outgoing ED's post-departure role stay undefined, which risks shadow leadership that undermines the successor's authority.
